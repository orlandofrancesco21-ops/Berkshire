# Memory semiconductors — value chain

Who does what, who captures value at each step, where moats sit, and where disintermediation risk lives. Companion to `primer.md` and `players.md`. Data conventions and verification labels as stated in `primer.md`.

## Value chain map

Equipment & materials → **Bit manufacture (the fab)** → Advanced packaging & test → Controller / firmware → Module & channel → Device OEM or hyperscaler → End user

- **Equipment & materials.** Lithography (ASML — single-vendor in EUV), etch and deposition (Lam Research, Applied Materials, Tokyo Electron), metrology (KLA), plus wafers, photoresist and process gases. Sells capital equipment against a capex budget, so its revenue is a *derivative of the memory cycle's second derivative* — it books orders when memory margins are highest and delivers 12-24 months later. Structurally the most profitable and least volatile position in the chain.
- **Bit manufacture — the fab.** Samsung, SK hynix, Micron, Kioxia, YMTC, CXMT, plus SanDisk's 49.9% claim on Flash Ventures output. This is where the cycle's entire P&L amplitude lives: gross margins have run from **-32.7%** (Micron, a FY2023 quarter, `partial`) to **84.6%** (Micron, quarter ended 2026-05-28, `partial`) inside three years on the same asset base. Fixed-cost absorption is the whole margin story; utilisation is the switch.
- **Advanced packaging & test.** For commodity DRAM and NAND this is a low-margin back-end step, often outsourced (SanDisk runs its own assembly/test in Penang, Malaysia, and holds 20% of the SDSS venture with JCET in Shanghai after selling 80% on 2024-09-28, with a five-year **$550M minimum annual** supply commitment running to 2029-09-28 — `verified`, SanDisk FY2025 10-K and 10-Q). For **HBM it is a genuine bottleneck**: TSV stacking and hybrid bonding are separately capacity-constrained, which is part of why adding DRAM wafers no longer adds proportional HBM bits. Samsung and SK hynix have reportedly committed ~$52bn to an HBM packaging hub (`unverified`).
- **Controller / firmware.** The layer that turns raw NAND into an SSD. Merchant controller vendors (Silicon Motion, Phison, Marvell) and in-house designs (Samsung, SanDisk, Micron, Solidigm). Modest revenue share, disproportionate influence on enterprise SSD qualification cycles, and the closest thing NAND has to a switching-cost surface — a qualified enterprise SSD platform delays substitution by quarters.
- **Module & channel.** Kingston, ADATA, Crucial (Micron's own brand), distributors and retail. Asset-light, buys bits and resells assemblies, economics entirely different from the fab: low gross margin, high inventory beta, makes its money on *timing* rather than on cost position. The layer that historically absorbed and amplified spot volatility.
- **Device OEM or hyperscaler.** Apple, Dell, HP, Lenovo, the phone makers, and increasingly the cloud service providers buying direct. In a shortage this layer is a price taker, and the elasticity shows up in *its* P&L first — Samsung's own MX/mobile division posted its first-ever operating loss (~KRW 0.7tn) in Q2 2026 on memory input costs (`partial`), which is the same company on both sides of the trade.
- **End user.** Consumer, enterprise, or an inference workload. Only the last of these has demand that scales with tokens rather than with unit counts, which is the entire structural bull argument.

## Who captures the economics

Ranges rather than points, because the split **rotates with the cycle** — that rotation is the single most important thing on this page. Shares below are my characterisation from the structure and the reported margins, not a sourced industry decomposition; treat them as `unverified` orders of magnitude.

| Stage | Revenue share of end bit spend | Profit share **at a shortage** (now) | Profit share **at a glut** | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equipment & materials | ~15-20% (of capex, not of bit revenue) | ~10-15% | **~60-100%+** | Sells against capex budgets, which lag price. At a trough the fabs lose money and the equipment vendors are the only profitable link |
| **Bit manufacture (fab)** | ~55-70% | **~75-85%** | **negative** | All the operating leverage, in both directions. This is the only stage whose profit share can be below zero |
| Advanced packaging & test | ~5-10% | ~5% commodity; materially higher for HBM | low but positive | Commodity back-end is a service business; HBM packaging is a real constraint and prices like one |
| Controller / firmware | ~3-5% | ~3-5% | positive and stable | Design-cycle revenue, not bit revenue — survives the down-leg |
| Module & channel | ~10-15% | ~2-5% | often negative | Makes money on timing; carries the inventory when price turns |
| Device OEM / hyperscaler | n/a (buyer) | pays | **captures** | The counterparty. Its margin is the mirror image of the fab's |

**The investable consequence:** memory's profit pool does not shrink at a trough — it *moves*, out of the fabs and into the equipment vendors and the device makers. A sector allocation that wants exposure to the demand without the price is an equipment allocation, and that is a different trade with a different entry rule, not a hedge.

## Moats by stage

- **Equipment — the deepest and most durable moat in the chain.** ASML's EUV position is single-vendor, and leading-edge DRAM cannot scale without it. This is MOAT-M3's shape in commercial rather than regulatory form: the constraint *is* the moat, and it is the reason DRAM has a supply chokepoint at all. Note the asymmetry that defines this sector: **NAND scales vertically, with etch and deposition tools available from three vendors, so it has no equivalent gate.** Anyone with capital and a cleanroom can add NAND bits; nobody can add leading-edge DRAM bits without joining an ASML queue.
- **Fab — cost position, and nothing else** (MOAT-P4). Scale in memory buys cost-per-bit rank. It does not buy pricing power, and in a downturn it buys a larger fixed-cost base. The relevant question is never absolute cost but *rank against the marginal producer* — and the marginal producer in NAND is increasingly a state-funded Chinese entrant that is indifferent to the price at which it clears.
- **HBM specifically has a qualification moat that commodity DRAM does not.** HBM is designed into an accelerator per-customer, qualified, and contracted multi-year. That is a genuine switching cost by MOAT-P1's test — leaving strands validation work the *customer* performed. It is also narrow: it binds the current accelerator generation, not the next.
- **Controllers and enterprise SSD platforms** carry a weaker version of the same qualification moat. Quarters, not years.
- **Module and channel: no moat.** Brand at retail is worth a few points of price to consumers and nothing to a hyperscaler.
- **What does NOT exist anywhere in this chain, and must not be imported from other frameworks** (NARC-M2 — an assumption carried in from the last industry you studied):
  - **Switching costs on a commodity die.** MOAT-P1 requires that leaving strands an asset the customer built. A DRAM die strands nothing. Qualification delays substitution by quarters, not years. Do not import file-format logic into a fungible bit.
  - **Network effects.** MOAT-P3 requires a named mechanism by which one customer's use makes the product more valuable to another. There is none in memory. Anyone asserting one is describing a standards body.
  - **"Recurring revenue is stable."** The new LTA book is the closest thing memory has ever had, and it is under a year old and untested through a down-leg.

## Disintermediation risk

- **Compression and architecture change attacking the demand leg directly.** Google's reported "TurboQuant" KV-cache compression (2026-03) is the live example — claimed ≥60% reduction in LLM memory footprint in one report and ~6x in another; **the two conflict and both are `unverified`**. This is the memory-specific form of VAL-T2: compression on cycle fear mean-reverts, compression on an architecture shift does not, and they look identical on a screen. It deserves a verified answer or an explicit kill switch, not a footnote.
- **HDD substituting for NAND at the capacity tier.** Nearline HDD is the direct substitute for high-capacity, low-IOPS storage and is currently *benefiting* from NAND scarcity (see `players.md` on why HDD is a different business). If NAND prices roll, the substitution runs back the other way.
- **CXL-attached memory and emerging non-volatile memory (MRAM, ReRAM)** as long-dated architecture risks to both DRAM and NAND. Not researched this session; flagged, not assessed.
- **The module and channel tier is being routed around right now.** Direct multi-year hyperscaler agreements with prepayments and financial guarantees move the buyer relationship to the fab and strip the distributor of its role. SanDisk's contract liabilities went $25M → $511M in nine months on customer advances (`verified`). This is disintermediation *by* the fab, which is why it reads as a bull point at the fab — but it also removes the shock absorber that historically sat between the fab and the spot market.
- **The fab layer itself is exposed to a buyer that becomes a producer.** Not currently a live risk in memory — no hyperscaler has attempted its own DRAM — but the HBM-plus-custom-accelerator direction of travel is worth watching.

## Vertical integration trends

- **HBM is pulling DRAM makers forward into advanced packaging**, because the packaging is the constraint. This creates value: it is integration *into* a bottleneck, which is the only kind that reliably does.
- **SanDisk integrated sideways into DRAM, and it is the one genuinely contestable capital-allocation decision in the sector right now.** On 2026-03-25 it agreed, and on **2026-04-08 paid $972M**, for 138,685,000 Nanya Technology shares — **~3.9% fully diluted, struck at a 15% discount to the 30-day average, locked up for three years**, bundled with a multi-year DRAM supply arrangement (`verified`, SanDisk 8-K 2026-03-25 and 10-Q). Read it plainly: an illiquid, locked, minority stake in a second-tier DRAM maker, funded at the top of SanDisk's own cycle, at a moment when the alternative use of $972M was its own equity. CAPA-P3 puts the burden of proof on the buyer above roughly a tenth of market cap — this is far below that threshold and so does not trip the rule, but CAPA-M2's test (does capital deployment move inversely to the multiple across a cycle?) is the one that bites, and it will not have an answer for years. **Reasonable people will disagree; a thesis should say so rather than assume it is good.**
- **NAND's JV structure is the anti-integration case, and it is instructive.** SanDisk owns half the tools and none of the buildings, takes ~50% of output, pays half of all fixed costs whether or not it lifts the wafers, must fund ~50% of JV capex when JV cash flow is short, and **may not manufacture flash itself**. On 2026-01-29 the three JVs were extended to co-terminate 2034-12-31, and SanDisk concurrently agreed to pay Kioxia **$1.2bn over 2026-2029** under an "Agreement to Enhance Collaboration" for continued supply availability (all `verified`, 10-Q Note 10). That is $1.2bn of cash with no corresponding asset, paid to lock in a structure that leaves the equity holder unable to cut fixed cost in a downturn. Its aggregate committed cash requirements at 2026-04-03 were **$12,793M, of which $6,543M is Flash Ventures related**, against **$134M of nine-month reported capex** (`verified`).

  **The reading that matters for any analyst looking at this sector:** a NAND participant's reported PP&E capex is not its capital intensity. SanDisk's $134M of nine-month capex sits alongside $6.5bn of JV commitments, $993M of sale-leaseback lease guarantees on JV tools, $856M of prepaid Kioxia building depreciation still to be credited and $373M more committed. **Anyone reading $134M as "asset-light" has misread the structure**, and the same shape will recur in any JV-based memory participant.
- **Conglomerate disposal has been the dominant direction of travel in NAND for twenty years** — Toshiba out of Kioxia, Intel out of NAND, Western Digital out of SanDisk. Vertical integration in this industry has more often been *undone* than built, and the disposals have generally been the right call for the seller.
