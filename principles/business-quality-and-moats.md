# Business quality and moats

**Scope:** What makes a franchise durable, how durability is actually tested, and how moats end. Covers the mechanism behind an advantage (an asset the customer cannot take with them, pricing power, genuine demand-side network effects, cost-curve position), the ways moats decay (architecture shifts, cohort replacement, under-funded moat maintenance, distribution given away voluntarily), and how policy-granted advantage differs from earned advantage. Not here: what a moat is *worth* (valuation discipline), whether the market currently believes in it (narrative and cycle models), or what management does with the cash it throws off (capital allocation signals) — those route elsewhere unless the point is the durability of the business itself.

## Principles

### MOAT-P1 — Lock-in must trap the customer's own asset

**MOAT-P1** — Credit switching-cost lock-in only when leaving destroys or strands an asset the *customer* built — their accumulated work in a standard the company controls, their history in a system they cannot export — not when leaving merely costs them retraining on the vendor's product. The first survives product cycles and management turnover; the second is repriced the moment a challenger is dramatically rather than marginally better.

### MOAT-P2 — Grade the moat on the last pricing action

**MOAT-P2** — Grade a moat on the most recent pricing action, not on the moat narrative: an increase taken and held without abnormal churn validates it, while a rollback, a quiet pause, discounting at renewal, or promotion on core rather than clearance product falsifies it. When the narrative and the pricing action disagree, the pricing action wins.

- **Voices:** **qualifies** — Owner's own analysis, `sectors/creative-and-design-software/companies/ADBE/thesis.md` 2026-07-17 debrief [unverified]: a pricing pause chosen to remove friction for new-cohort acquisition graded PARTIAL rather than triggered — the letter of the test fired while its spirit did not (holds when: management names the trade-off in advance and the acquired cohort's conversion is measurable within a few quarters; if the deferral outlives that window while conversion disappoints, treat it as a rollback)

### MOAT-P3 — A network effect needs a named demand-side mechanism

**MOAT-P3** — Credit a network effect only when you can name the mechanism by which one customer's use makes the product more valuable to another customer; compatibility with a shared file, purchasing or processing scale, and proximity to a cultural moment are not network effects. Absent that mechanism, do not underwrite winner-take-most terminal share or the multiple that assumes it.

### MOAT-P4 — Undifferentiated output means cost-curve position is the moat

**MOAT-P4** — Where output is undifferentiated, the only durable advantage is cost position — the ability to earn at the price that forces the marginal producer out, whether that price is set by a commodity market or reset annually by the buyer; if you cannot state where the company sits on the relevant cost curve, you do not yet have a view on quality. The inverse holds too: in a differentiated business, driving for lowest cost destroys the moat, because the cost being cut is what the customer is paying for.

### MOAT-P5 — Growth-stage quality is a unit-economics screen, cleared before the story counts

**MOAT-P5** — For a growth-stage company, check the unit economics before the narrative gets any weight: real scale, fast growth reached at or near breakeven, high gross margin, recurring revenue that customers keep renewing, no customer concentration, and growth bought cheaply in cash. A growth company that fails most of that screen is being valued on its story, whatever the story is.
`[evidence: emerging | my record: untested]` (as-of 2026-11-16)

- **Voices:** **agrees** — Lead Edge Capital, "The Lead Edge 8", leadedge.com, read 2026-11-16 [verified]: a growth equity firm (self-described, $9bn AUM, $50-400M checks into private and public technology companies) seeks companies with most of eight criteria: revenue $10M+, 25%+ YoY growth, 70%+ gross margins, breakeven or profitable, SaaS or highly recurring revenue, 90%+ gross retention, a diversified customer base, and capital efficiency measured as scale relative to cash burn (holds when: the company is GROWTH-STAGE, meaning revenue growing roughly 20%+ a year at or near breakeven. Do not apply it to mature low-growth franchises, where 25% growth is the wrong bar and its absence says nothing about quality; to early-stage companies still burning cash, which fail it by construction; or to cyclicals and balance-sheet financials. The thresholds are set for software and tech-enabled businesses, so the 70% gross-margin bar misreads hardware, consumer and services models. "Most" is unquantified on the firm's own pages)

## Mental models

### MOAT-M1 — Incumbency buys time, not destiny

**MOAT-M1** — On an architecture shift, the installed base sets the *timing* of decline rather than whether it happens, and the payoff is asymmetric: an incumbent loses more from being on the wrong side of the shift than it gains from holding lock-in through it. Underwrite the shift outcome separately from the lock-in annuity instead of netting one against the other.
`[evidence: emerging | my record: untested]` (as-of 2026-07-28)

- **Voices:** **agrees** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/creative-and-design-software.md` 2026-05-20 [unverified]: workflow lock-in is real and architecture shifts are real, and they are not symmetric — the installed base buys time but not destiny, and scale in the old architecture can be a millstone rather than a defence (holds when: the shift changes how the work is done; a shift that only makes the same work cheaper is absorbed by the incumbent far more often than it displaces one)
- **Voices:** **qualifies** — Clayton Christensen / disruption theory, as cited in `frameworks/creative-and-design-software.md` "Mental-model crossover risks" 2026-05-20 [unverified]: the vault cites the playbook as bottom-up in order to reject its clean application — pressure arrived at the low end and the high end at once (holds when: you assess both ends; assuming bottom-up sequencing sets the clock too late and looks at the wrong competitors — and nobody here has read the primary source, so this is a pointer to a framework worth absorbing, not a characterization of what its author argued)
- **My record:** 2026-07-28 — untested in outcome, but the vault has committed capital inside a live architecture-shift debate (ADBE, bought against a bear case that the shift is real — `journal/2026-07-17-decision.md`), so this lens will be graded whether or not it is invoked.

### MOAT-M2 — Split the moat into the trained cohort and the next cohort

**MOAT-M2** — Separate the part of a moat that binds customers already trained or already loyal from the part that binds the *next* cohort: only the second compounds, and the first is an annuity whose life is no longer than that cohort's. Because reported financials can run for years on the annuity alone, the question to ask of a terminal value is how much of it is being paid for by cohorts not yet acquired.
`[evidence: emerging | my record: mixed]` (as-of 2026-07-28)

- **Voices:** **agrees** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/branded-athletic-footwear-and-apparel.md` "Common bear-case archetypes" 2026-05-20 [unverified]: the "lost the kids" bear archetype is the same structure in a consumer brand — cohort preference surveys turn several years before revenue does (holds when: the erosion persists across multiple survey periods rather than one; a single cohort's fashion swing is noise)

### MOAT-M3 — Where returns are policy-granted, the regime is both moat and ceiling

**MOAT-M3** — Where returns depend on a licence, a rate base, an approved network, or trade protection, the regulatory regime *is* the moat — and the same regime caps the return it protects and can withdraw it by decision rather than by competition. Treat policy-granted advantage as high-certainty and low-ceiling, and note that scale enlarges the regulatory target at roughly the rate it lowers unit cost.
`[evidence: emerging | my record: untested]` (as-of 2026-07-28)

- **Voices:** **agrees** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/us-managed-care-and-health-services.md` 2026-05-20 [unverified]: scale creates the cost advantage and the enforcement target simultaneously, and regulatory exposure is the dominant variable outside underwriting itself — an operator that runs compliance as a brake on growth is buying durability (holds when: the profit pool is large enough to be politically visible; a small licensed operator carries the moat without the target)
- **Voices:** **agrees** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/energy-and-cleantech.md` 2026-05-08 [unverified]: for Western manufacturers facing structurally cheaper imports, policy is the moat or there isn't one — and returns that require a subsidy stack with a sunset clause should be modelled unsubsidised (holds when: the protection is durable across administrations; where it is not, the honest base case is the unprotected one)
- **My record:** 2026-07-28 — untested in outcome. The nearest thing to a decision is the UNH pass at initiation (`sectors/us-managed-care-and-health-services/companies/UNH/thesis.md`, 2026-05-21), where an unresolved regulatory overhang on the most profitable segment was one reason the asymmetry was judged insufficient — a pass, so it produced no result to learn from.

## Traps

### MOAT-T1 — Inferring the moat from the metrics it produced

**MOAT-T1** — The moat is being asserted from its own outcomes — high margin, high share, high retention, growing adjacency revenue — with no mechanism named anywhere in the argument. Two checks: delete every trailing metric and see whether a moat is still described; and for any claimed moat *extension*, require the segment's operating income to grow faster than its revenue, because revenue-only growth means the rent is being paid rather than captured.
`[evidence: emerging | my record: untested]` (as-of 2026-07-28)

- **Voices:** **agrees** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/us-managed-care-and-health-services.md` 2026-05-20 [unverified]: the test that separates genuine vertical integration from optical revenue is whether the services segment's operating income grows faster than its revenue; done badly, integration just adds revenue at zero or negative margin (holds when: the segment is mature enough that build-out spend is not structurally suppressing its margin — a genuinely investing segment fails this test by design for a while, so put a date on when it must pass)
- **Voices:** **agrees** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/creative-and-design-software.md` 2026-05-20 [unverified]: retention above the healthy threshold is a coincident-to-slightly-leading indicator, not a guarantee — it is true until it isn't, and when it rolls over it rolls over fast (holds when: the metric is disclosed on a consistent basis; a metric the company stops publishing cannot be the evidence, and its absence is itself a signal)
- **My record:** 2026-07-28 — untested as a check I have run deliberately. Adjacent evidence that it matters: the ADBE 2026-07-17 debrief found two monitoring observables dead because the disclosure basis changed, which is the same failure one step downstream — an argument resting on a metric rather than a mechanism loses the argument when the metric goes away.

### MOAT-T2 — Cutting the moat's maintenance spend flatters the metrics that describe it

**MOAT-T2** — Margin expansion earned by cutting the spend that maintains the moat — research in a technology arms race, demand creation and product innovation in a brand, cost-curve investment in a commodity business — reads as operating quality on the income statement and is decay. Test every margin improvement by asking which line fell and whether that line is the moat's maintenance capex.
`[evidence: emerging | my record: untested]` (as-of 2026-07-28)

- **Voices:** **agrees** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/creative-and-design-software.md` 2026-05-20 [unverified]: research sits in operating expense but functions as the capex of a software business, and names that under-invest to flatter margins are the canonical decay trap (holds when: the spend is genuinely the arms race; overhead cut without touching the arms-race line is real operating improvement and should be credited)
- **Voices:** **qualifies** — Owner's own analysis, `frameworks/branded-athletic-footwear-and-apparel.md` 2026-05-20 [unverified]: the input has an output metric — share of revenue from recently launched product — so spend that rises without revenue follow-through is mis-firing and can be cut while durability improves (holds when: you judge on the output metric rather than the input; a company can be both over-spending and under-innovating)

### MOAT-T3 — The distribution moat given away voluntarily

**MOAT-T3** — A distribution moat can be dismantled by its owner rather than by a competitor: exiting channels, cutting partners, or disintermediating the shelf to capture their margin. When a company voluntarily narrows the distribution it controls, treat it as moat destruction until end-demand sell-through — not shipped revenue — proves otherwise, because that asset takes years to build and one strategy cycle to lose.

- **Voices:** **qualifies** — Owner's own analysis, `sectors/branded-athletic-footwear-and-apparel/companies/NKE/thesis.md` 2026-07-17 debrief [unverified]: deliberately pulling supply and withdrawing promotion looked identical on the revenue line and was moat repair, not surrender (holds when: partner reorders and full-price realization improve *while* the withdrawal is happening — that is the discriminator between cleaning up distribution and losing it)
