# S&P 500 — asset-class exposure framework

> Personal playbook for forming and defending an **expected real return range** for the S&P 500 (US large-cap, market-cap-weighted, single-country equity), and for judging an allocation to it against its alternatives.
> Written 2026-08-23, with the Shiller CAPE at ~41 (2026-08, `library/shiller-ie-data.md`, verified) against a full-series mean near 17 — read every dated figure below as calibration, not as current state, and re-pull anything you are about to act on.
> Append-only in the learned log. Refine every time a fund tracking this exposure is deep-dived, and every time a realized 10-year window closes on a range this file produced.

**Last updated:** 2026-08-23
**Exposure class:** listed equity, United States, large cap, free-float market-cap weighted, committee-selected
**Decision unit:** allocation over a stated horizon, against named alternatives — **not** a buy/pass on a business
**Default horizon:** **10 years.** Not a preference. It is the only horizon at which the anchors below have measurable explanatory power (Vanguard 2012 CAPE R² 0.43 at 10 years vs 0.12 at 1 year, verified via `frameworks/developed-market-equity.md`), and the file refuses to quote an expected return without it.
**Umbrella framework:** no — decision-grade for a broad S&P 500 tracker. The S&P 500 is one exposure with one return decomposition and one anchor family; it does not need a sub-exposure split. A US *total-market* fund (adds mid/small), a US *equal-weight* fund (removes the cap concentration), a *sector* or *factor* fund, and a *global* or *ex-US* fund are each a different exposure needing its own framework, not a sub-section of this one.

## Scope — what this exposure actually is

The gap between the label and the holdings, stated first, because everything downstream is arithmetic on the holdings and rhetoric on the label. **The single most important thing about this exposure relative to its sibling `developed-market-equity.md`: there is no "World" euphemism to strip away — the label already says US. The trap here is the opposite one, that "500 companies" sounds diversified when the top handful dominate.**

- **Index universe:** the S&P 500 — the ~500 leading US companies chosen by the S&P Dow Jones **Index Committee**. This framework covers that index and its exact trackers (VUAG/VUSA, CSPX/VUSD, SXR8, VOO, SPY, IVV). It does **not** cover MSCI USA or the Russell 1000: those are different large-cap US indices with rules-based selection and different constituent counts, and a fund tracking one of them routes to its own framework, not here.
- **Selection rule:** committee discretion within published guardrails — US domicile, a float-adjusted market-cap minimum (raised over time; USD ~$18bn band in recent methodology, **partial**, pull the current S&P methodology PDF), adequate liquidity and public float, and a **GAAP-profitability screen** (positive most-recent quarter and positive trailing four quarters). The committee decides *when* a qualifying name is added — the multi-year delay before Tesla's 2020 inclusion is the standard illustration that this is not a mechanical index.
- **Weighting:** free-float market cap. The mechanical consequence, same as any cap-weighted index: the exposure buys more of whatever has already gone up. That is why the concentration below is a *result* of price action, not a decision anyone made — and why it is currently at a record.
- **Explicitly included:** only US-domiciled large caps. Eleven GICS sectors, of which Information Technology is the largest (~33%, **partial** — pull the current S&P factsheet).
- **Explicitly excluded:** **everything non-US** (no Taiwan, no European or Japanese names), **all mid and small caps** (that is the S&P 500 vs the S&P Completion / total-market difference), and any name failing the profitability or float screen. A holder who thinks they own "the US market" owns the large-cap top of it only — the S&P 500 is ~80% of US market cap but a much smaller share of US *companies*.
- **Rebalancing and reconstitution:** quarterly rebalancing of float weights; additions and deletions announced by the committee as they occur, not on a fixed calendar. Which makes the boundary of this exposure a **committee decision, not a market fact** — more discretionary than MSCI's rules-based reviews, and worth one paragraph of attention, not a research budget.

## What drives long-run returns for this asset class

**The identity the valuation file will use.** Expected annualised real return over the horizon, in local (USD) terms, before the wrapper:

> **starting shareholder yield** (dividend yield + net buyback yield) **+ real growth in earnings per share + annualised change in the valuation multiple − fund costs − tax drag** (± currency, for a EUR-based holder)

The first three terms approximate an accounting identity, not a model: they sum to the return by construction if the multiple term is measured on the same earnings definition as the yield. Everything arguable lives in the growth term and the multiple term. The last two have a certain sign.

### What each term has historically contributed

This exposure is where the cleanest sourced decomposition available to the vault is **native rather than a proxy**: Ibbotson and Chen's identity is for **US equities 1926–2000** (Yale ICF Working Paper 00-44, March 2002, verified via `frameworks/developed-market-equity.md` — the PDF was read there), and the S&P 500 and its spliced predecessors *are* that US series. It reconstructs the 10.70% compounded annual nominal return as:

| Term | Contribution, US 1926–2000 | Can it repeat? |
|---|---|---|
| Income (dividend) return | **4.28 pp** | **No, not at this level.** The S&P 500's dividend yield today is roughly 1.2–1.3% (**partial** — pull the factsheet), below even MSCI World's 1.53%. The single largest reason a naive "US equities return 7% real" assumption is unsafe today |
| Real earnings-per-share growth | **1.75 pp** | Bounded. Per-share growth in the real economy; Straehl–Ibbotson argue per-share payouts grew with productivity while aggregate payouts grew with GDP (FAJ 73(3), 2017 — **partial**, paywalled/unread) |
| Change in the P/E multiple | **1.25 pp** | **No.** The P/E went 10.22 → 25.96 over the window (verified via sibling). A multiple cannot re-rate forever; this term borrows from the future, and today's starting multiple is far above the 2000 endpoint |
| Inflation | 3.08 pp | Pass-through; strip it, this framework works in real terms |
| Reinvestment return | 0.20 pp | Small, an artifact of the arithmetic |

That implies a **compounded real return of roughly 7.4% per year for US equities 1926–2000** (1.1070 / 1.0308 − 1). One load-bearing caveat that differs from the sibling: **for the S&P 500 there is no "it's only one country, the world did worse" discount to apply — this IS the one country.** The 7.4% is the survivor market's own record, and the honest question is not "does the world match the US" but "does the US future match the US past" — which is a regime question (below), not a survivorship correction.

- **The dividend term has partly migrated, not disappeared.** Buybacks now deliver a large share of what dividends used to, and US large caps are the most buyback-intensive cohort on earth. Shiller's dataset added a total-return CAPE in September 2018 specifically to correct the EPS-growth bias from changing distribution policy (`library/shiller-ie-data.md`, verified). Any decomposition using a bare dividend yield **and** a bare EPS growth rate on modern S&P data is double-counting the shift. **Use shareholder yield, or use TR-CAPE, and say which.**

### Which term dominates when

Over ten years the yield and growth terms dominate and the multiple term is a swing factor. Over one year the multiple term is essentially the entire return. That asymmetry is the whole reason this framework insists on the horizon: the same identity produces a usable forecast at 10 years and noise at one.

## Valuation anchors and their honest track record

The core of the file. Because the S&P 500 is the exact series Shiller's CAPE is built on, **every anchor here is native, not borrowed** — the Vanguard R² figures were computed on US data and apply to this exposure without a substitution caveat. Explanatory power is stated with its sample, and **no anchor appears without the horizon at which it fails.**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| **Trailing 1-year P/E** | ~10 years | 1–3 years | **0.38** — statistically indistinguishable from CAPE | same | same |
| **Any smoothing P/E1…P/E15** | ~10 years | 1–3 years | R² **0.38–0.43**; "no clear winner" | same | same |
| **Best of any signal at 1 year** | — | **1 year** | **0.12**; most metrics near zero | same | same |

| **Trailing dividend yield alone** | — | both | explained **<20%** of 10-year returns | same | same |
| **Damodaran implied ERP** (solve for the discount rate equating price to PV of expected cash flows) | ~forward, price-based | not a timing tool | forward-looking; a genuinely *independent* second lens | US, 2025 | pages.stern.nyu.edu (verified via sibling) |

**The conditional record, computed natively from the absorbed Shiller file** (`library/shiller-ie-data.md`, verified — this is US large-cap = this exposure, not a proxy):

- **10-year forward real total return by starting-CAPE quintile:** cheapest quintile (CAPE 4.8–15.4) **median +9.0%**, range −4.2% to +20.0%; most expensive quintile (CAPE 18.0–44.2) **median +4.8%**, range −5.9% to +13.1%. Seven independent decades behind each bucket.
- **The between-bucket gap is 4.2 pts/yr; the spread inside the most expensive bucket is 19 pts.** Starting valuation moves the centre of the distribution and barely narrows it. This is the single most important calibration in the file.
- **The five most expensive starting months in the entire 145-year record all produced NEGATIVE real returns over the following decade** — CAPE 43.2–44.2, clustered 1999-07 to 2000-04, delivering −2.55% to −4.59% real per year for ten years (verified). Today's ~41 sits just below that cluster, at the 99th percentile. The honest reading is not "therefore −3%": it is that **the record contains no example of a decade starting this expensive that ended well, and only five examples at all.**

### Why this asset class has the predictability it has

The S&P 500 is an equity index, so it fixes **nothing** at purchase. Unlike a bond — whose starting yield to maturity essentially *is* the return — the S&P 500 buyer forecasts an unknown earnings stream, an unknown payout policy, and, decisively, an unknown terminal multiple at which someone else buys. Two of the three decomposition terms are forecasts and the third (the multiple) is a forecast about other people's future forecasts.

A CAPE reading is therefore **not** the equity analogue of a bond yield. It is a conditioning variable that shifts a wide distribution; the width of that distribution is the finding, not a measurement defect. The anchor sets the *centre* of the range and the historical dispersion sets the *width* — and the width is enormous (per the 19-pt within-bucket spread above). What ends the mean reversion is the open question of whether the CAPE's own mean has permanently shifted (regime section below); the anchor assumes the multiple reverts toward a historical level, and that level may no longer be the right target.

## Historical return distribution

Point estimates are for people who have not read this section. **These figures are computed natively on the S&P 500's own series**, `scripts/exposure-history.py`, run 2026-08-23 — see the proxy section for exactly which tickers and their biases.

- **10-year annualised REAL total return** (VFINX, 1980–2026, CPI 2.9%, ~4.7 independent windows): **worst −7.3%, p10 +0.7%, median +8.9%, p90 +13.0%, best +16.5%; 9% of windows ended below zero** (computed 2026-08-23).
- **10-year annualised NOMINAL, deep price-only history** (`^GSPC`, 1927–2026, ~9.9 independent windows, **no dividends**): worst −10.0%, p10 −0.2%, median +7.5%, best +17.2%; 11% below zero (computed 2026-08-23). Read this for *shape and breadth of sample*, not for return level — it omits the dividend term.
- **What CAPE explains, and what it leaves:** even conditioning on starting valuation, ~60% of the historical variation in long-term real returns is unexplained (Vanguard 2012, verified via sibling), and the signal lives in the tails — regressing only the middle 50% of CAPE readings collapses R² from 0.43 to 0.20. Whether today's ~41 is an "extreme" is the first question: it is, at the 99th percentile.

## Regime and structural-change risks

Why the historical record may not repeat. Each with its direction of bias.

- **The CAPE's own mean has shifted, and nobody agrees why.** CAPE averaged **14.6 over 1926–1984**; since then it has "defied mean reversion, having only once dropped below its long-run average" (Davis et al., fair-value CAPE, *JPM* 44(3) 2018, verified via sibling). Today's ~41 sits against a full-series mean near 17 and within striking distance of the December 1999 all-time high of 44.19. If the level shift is structural, every historical CAPE-to-return regression is biased pessimistic today; if it is not, the current reading is near a generational peak. **This framework does not know which, and says so.**
- **US exceptionalism is the whole distribution.** Unlike the World framework, this file cannot hedge the survivor problem by pointing at a lower world number — the S&P 500 *is* the survivor. The Yearbook's US real ~6.6% (1900–2024) against the world's 5.2% is, for this exposure, not a "your proxy is optimistic" caveat but a **direct statement that the base case investors carry in their head is the best equity market of the century extrapolated forward.** Direction: optimistic if US relative outperformance mean-reverts, and it has already: global equities returned only 3.5% real annualised since 2000 (verified via sibling).
- **Record concentration.** Top-ten weight at a record high (needs the current factsheet, partial) means index-level returns now depend on a handful of mega-cap outcomes to a degree the diversified historical record did not. This is not itself a forecast, but it means the historical dispersion figures — drawn from a less concentrated index — may understate the tail. Marks' December 2025 memo "Is it a bubble?" (`library/marks-2025-12-is-it-a-bubble.md`, absorbed) is the vault's on-point reading here and should be cited in any fund document leaning on the concentration point.
- **Accounting changes in the denominator.** Siegel (2016) argues CAPE's secular rise is partly an artifact of accounting-standard changes, proposing NIPA earnings instead (reported in the fair-value CAPE paper, verified as that paper's characterisation; Siegel's own paper unread — claim verified, substance partial). Direction: makes reported CAPE look higher than a like-for-like historical comparison would.
- **Payout-policy shift.** US large caps are the most buyback-intensive equities; a bare dividend yield understates shareholder return and a bare EPS series overstates growth if buybacks are mishandled. Shiller's TR-CAPE exists for exactly this (verified).
- **Committee-selection drift.** The S&P 500 is not rules-based: the profitability screen and committee timing mean the index's character is shaped by discretionary decisions (e.g. the delayed Tesla inclusion). A historical multiple for "the S&P 500" is an average of a differently-selected thing.

## Correlation and portfolio role

- **Role:** the growth engine. Not ballast, and must never be sized as if it were.
- **Stock-bond correlation, long run:** ~0.19 in the US, 0.33 across countries (UBS/DMS Yearbook 2025, verified via sibling); negative from the late 1990s to 2021 and then not. The diversification benefit of a bond sleeve is real on the century average and **regime-dependent** in the window a real allocation lives in — do not size a bond sleeve on the post-1998 correlation.
- **Overlap with what is already owned:** NKE and ADBE are S&P 500 constituents. The weight overlap is small but the *factor* overlap (US large cap) is total — an S&P 500 allocation is more US-large-cap beta on top of two US-large-cap single stocks. State the combined exposure in the fund document rather than asserting diversification.
- **Currency:** 100% USD for a EUR-based holder, unhedged by default — higher than the World fund's 72%. Over 10 years currency is closer to noise than a return driver; over the 1–3 year windows people actually judge themselves in, it can dominate. Hedged vs unhedged share class is a genuine allocation decision and belongs in the fund document, informed by this 100% weight.

## When does the valuation anchor lie?

The failure modes of this file's own method, written by the person most likely to be fooled by them.

- **When the horizon is short.** The anchor is not a signal at 1–3 years (R² ~0.12). Quoting a CAPE-derived number as a reason to wait for a better entry is using a 10-year instrument as a timing tool, which the evidence supports in neither direction.
- **When the reading is in the middle of its distribution.** R² falls to 0.20 on the middle 50% of readings. Today's ~41 is *not* mid-range — it is a 99th-percentile extreme, which is precisely where CAPE says the most. That cuts both ways: the anchor is at its most informative here, and what it says is "the record holds no good decade from a start this expensive."
- **When the anchor's own out-of-sample record is worse than doing nothing.** Since ~1985, CAPE-based out-of-sample forecasts have had larger average errors than simply using the trailing historical average (fair-value CAPE paper, verified). The reconciliation: CAPE **ranks decades well and levels them badly** — correlation of predicted vs realised 10-year returns 83% since 1960, 91% since 1985, yet persistently too pessimistic on the *level*. It knows which decade will be worse; it does not know by how much. This is the strongest single argument against acting mechanically on the ~41 reading.
- **When "expensive" has already persisted for a decade.** CAPE has dropped below its long-run average only once since 1985. An investor who sold on "expensive" waited out most of a generation. VAL-T2 at index level: name the *cause* of the elevated multiple before treating it as a forecast, because a structural cause and a cyclical one look identical on the chart.
- **When the denominator is not comparable.** Accounting-standard changes, buyback-driven EPS growth, and a ~one-third-technology, committee-selected composition mean today's numerator and denominator are not the 1926 objects. VAL-T1: a multiple is not information until the denominator is normalised, and "normalised" is a harder claim at index level than at single-stock level.
- **When the multiple term is quietly doing all the work.** Historically the multiple contributed ~1.25pp of a ~7.4pp real return. If a build has it contributing more, the build is a forecast about sentiment wearing valuation's clothes. Per VAL-P4, move the growth path and exit multiple together rather than pretending they are independent.

## Red flags at exposure level

- **A single-point expected return.** VAL-P1 governs: the output is a range over a stated horizon, never a price target.
- **"US equities return ~7% real" quoted as a law.** It is the survivor market's own past, and the first quarter of this century has already printed 3.5% real globally. For the S&P 500 there is no lower "world" number to fall back on — the optimism is baked into the definition.
- **Diversification asserted from the constituent count.** 500 names with a record top-ten weight is a different object from 500 equally-weighted names. Cite the top-ten weight, not the count.
- **A backtest that starts after 1985.** Every such sample contains the CAPE level shift and a bond bull market and flatters both legs of a 60/40.
- **A dividend yield used as the shareholder-yield term.** Understates payout badly for the most buyback-intensive cohort on earth.
- **Treating the ~41 CAPE as either "therefore crash" or "this time is different".** The honest output is a wide, valuation-shifted *range* whose centre is lower than history's average and whose width is enormous — not a call.
- **Any monitoring trigger keyed to a vendor series that could be discontinued or re-methodologised** (PROD-T3). A CAPE series scraped from a third-party site has the same fragility as any vendor feed — name the fallback (the absorbed Shiller file, recomputed) next to the trigger or the trigger is decorative.

**Deliberately NOT switches here, so a future reader does not re-add them:** a drawdown of any depth (the table above shows depth carries no information about what follows); CAPE crossing any particular level (0.20 R² mid-distribution, and a documented 40-year one-way persistence — even at today's 99th-percentile reading the switch would be a *timing* call the evidence does not support); a bad year, or three; and any price level whatsoever. **Exit is never triggered by price alone** — a non-negotiable of this vault, not a preference of this file.

## What this framework does not cover

- **Wrapper mechanics.** Replication (physical full vs sampled vs synthetic), UCITS domicile, accumulating vs distributing, securities lending and its revenue split, counterparty exposure, fund size and closure risk, spread and premium/discount, and tracking difference all live in the universal vehicle lens, [`frameworks/etf-vehicle-mechanics.md`](etf-vehicle-mechanics.md), read alongside this file on every fund deep-dive. The best wrapper on a bad exposure is still a bad allocation; a good exposure through a leaky wrapper gives back the premium it earned.
- **Any single fund's costs.** The cost term is generic here; it gets its value from the fund's own documents. (S&P 500 trackers are among the cheapest funds in existence — VUAG's TER is single-digit basis points — which is a fund fact, not an exposure fact.)
- **The tax rules themselves** — see below for the boundary.

### Tax — the one exposure-specific wrinkle, plus a pointer

**This is research, not advice** — the specifics must be confirmed by Francesco's *Steuerberater* before any allocation rests on them. The full German-resident mechanics (§ 20 InvStG **Teilfreistellung** of 30% for an Aktienfonds, the Vorabpauschale on accumulating funds, Abgeltungsteuer) are identical across equity ETFs and are documented once in `frameworks/developed-market-equity.md` — not restated here.

What is **specific to this exposure** and therefore belongs in this file: **100% of the S&P 500's dividends are US-sourced**, so US dividend withholding tax applies to the *entire* income term, not a slice of it as in a World fund. For an Irish-domiciled UCITS ETF (VUAG is Irish-domiciled, ISIN prefix IE) the US–Ireland treaty reduces that withholding to **15%** at the fund level, versus a higher statutory rate — which is a large part of why Irish domicile dominates European S&P 500 trackers. The *rate and mechanism* are wrapper/domicile mechanics (vehicle lens); what belongs here is only the fact that because the exposure is 100% US-dividend, the domicile's US treaty rate applies to the whole income term rather than a fraction. As always, this is a property of the asset class stated for the fund document to price, not tax advice.

## Mental-model crossover risks

The assumptions from this vault's industry frameworks that do not transfer to an exposure. All five industry frameworks are single-business frameworks; importing their instincts into an asset class is the predictable failure — see NARC-M2.

- **Moat analysis is silent, and silence is not a clean bill.** No mechanism to name, no switching cost to credit, no cost curve to rank. An index owns the moats and the moatless together. As in `frameworks/memory-semiconductors.md`, a diagnostic that is simply inapplicable must not be read as "passed".
- **Management quality does not exist as a variable.** What replaces it is index methodology and the S&P Index Committee — worth one paragraph, not a CEO's research budget.
- **"Cheap" means something weaker.** At single-stock level a low multiple invites a search for the specific reason and can resolve; at index level the multiple is an aggregate whose components move in different directions and the causal story is unverifiable by construction. VAL-T2 — name the cause before acting, and accept you frequently cannot at index level.
- **The range should be WIDER than a single-stock range, not narrower.** The counter-intuitive one. Diversification removes idiosyncratic dispersion from the *realised* outcome, which feels like precision; it does nothing about the *forecast* dispersion — ~60% of 10-year variation unexplained, 19-pt within-bucket spread even after conditioning on valuation. A company deep-dive narrows its range by understanding one business; no amount of understanding narrows this one, because the residual is the market's future opinion of itself.
- **VAL-P3's three-independent-lenses rule is harder than it looks.** CAPE, trailing P/E, dividend yield and the building-block model are not four lenses — they share the earnings series and the mean-reversion assumption (Vanguard found them statistically indistinguishable). A genuinely independent second lens has to come from elsewhere: Damodaran's implied-ERP approach (forward-looking, price-based) is one; the absorbed Shiller conditional-distribution computation is another, because it grades the anchor against outcomes rather than restating it.
- **VAL-M1 does not apply.** There is no single cyclical trough to normalise past at index level; smoothing is already what CAPE does, and stacking a second normalisation double-counts.
- **The kill-switch instinct mostly fails here.** PROD-P3's requirements are easy for a company (a disclosed metric, a print date) and hard for an exposure. Most of what one wants to make a switch of at index level is price, and price alone is never a trigger. Accept a shorter switch list rather than manufacturing observable-looking ones.

- **What is the current top-ten weight and Information Technology weight, from the S&P factsheet?** Stated as partial above; a pulled factsheet turns them verified and sets the concentration kill-switch threshold.
- **What is the actual net buyback yield for the S&P 500, and where is it published?** Not established. Without it the shareholder-yield term is guesswork and one kill switch is unusable.
- **How should a CAPE-implied range be combined with a USD/EUR currency view over 10 years for a EUR-based holder?** Unresolved, and more acute here (100% USD) than for the World fund (72%).
- **This framework's own track record is n = 0.** It has never produced a range graded against a realised outcome. Treat it as well-reasoned rather than validated, and say so in any fund document that leans on it.
